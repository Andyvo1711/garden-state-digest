A client who picks a specific look from the samples is trusting the salon to deliver that look, or to say honestly if it cannot. This report on L.A. Perfection Nails is about a chrome set that came out nothing like chrome, and about how the result was handled afterward. Both the technique and the response are worth looking at, because each points to a habit that builds or breaks trust.

## Chrome That Wasn't Chrome

The client walked in, asked for the chrome nail samples, and chose one, then was told the set would cost $60, which she was happy to pay. The problem was the finish: the nails came out completely non-shiny, with none of the mirror-like look that defines chrome. A true chrome finish depends on a specific technique, a fully cured no-wipe top coat with the chrome powder rubbed in and buffed to a shine, then sealed. When the result is dull, it usually means a step in that process did not go right. Since the client selected the look from the salon's own samples, delivering that shine, or being upfront that it could not be matched that day, is exactly what she was counting on.

## Blaming the Lighting Instead of Owning It

The response is what turned a fixable letdown into a lost customer. Rather than acknowledging the finish had missed, the client felt the issue was blamed on the lighting, and then oil was applied to the nails, which she said only made things worse. Most of all, she felt dismissed by being told the nails looked about the same as the sample, when to her the difference was obvious. A client can tell chrome from dull, and being told otherwise reads as not being taken seriously. The trust-building move is to own the result plainly, something like "this did not come out the way it should, let me make it right," rather than explaining it away.

## Editorial Perspective

The encouraging part is that both halves of this are within a salon's control. First, know your techniques, and be honest about limits: if a particular look like chrome is offered on the samples, the team should be confident delivering it, and if it cannot be done well that day, saying so upfront lets the client choose before paying. Second, master the chrome process itself, since a proper base, cured top coat, well-buffed powder, and a clean seal are what create the mirror finish. Third, own a result that misses: acknowledge it honestly, offer to redo or refund, and never dismiss what the client can plainly see. Handled with the right technique or an honest heads-up, and a gracious response, this $60 visit could have delivered the chrome she picked, or kept her trust even if it needed a second try.
