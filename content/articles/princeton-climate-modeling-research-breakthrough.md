A Princeton University-led research team published a new climate modeling framework this month capable of predicting regional flooding risk with significantly greater precision than tools currently used by coastal planners along the Jersey Shore and broader mid-Atlantic coastline.

The model combines historical storm data, sea-level rise projections, and localized soil absorption data to generate flood-risk maps at a much finer geographic resolution than existing federal models, which researchers say often treat entire counties as uniform risk zones despite significant block-by-block variation.

"Existing models tell a town they're at moderate flood risk, when in reality one neighborhood might be in serious danger and another three blocks away is almost entirely safe," said lead researcher and Princeton civil engineering professor Helena Marsh. "That kind of blunt instrument leads to bad zoning decisions and wasted mitigation spending."

The research was conducted in partnership with two New Jersey coastal municipalities that provided historical flooding data in exchange for early access to the refined risk maps. Princeton says several additional Jersey Shore towns have already requested access to the model ahead of its planned public release next year.

The study's publication comes as New Jersey continues to grapple with the long-term costs of coastal flooding, with state officials citing improved risk modeling as a priority for future infrastructure and insurance policy decisions.
