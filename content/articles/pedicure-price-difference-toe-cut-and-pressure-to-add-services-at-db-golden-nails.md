A customer shared a review of DB GOLDEN NAILS that begins with loyalty. She says she had been a client for a long time, and that the technician was friendly at first. That history matters, because the people who stay with a salon the longest are often the ones who notice small changes in the experience. By her account, this visit ended with her deciding it would be her last.

## Price Expectations for a Gel Pedicure

The customer says she was getting a gel pedicure that she understood should cost $40, and that she was charged $57. She also notes that she came in only for a polish change.

Price differences like this are among the most common sources of friction at the register. When the final total is higher than a client expects, the cause is often an add-on or a different service category that was never discussed. The simplest protection is to confirm the service and price before work begins, for example, "A gel polish change on your toes, $40, correct?" If anything changes during the visit, a quick mention at the chair keeps the client informed. A visible price list helps returning clients as well, since prices and service names can shift over time.

## A Cut During the Service

The customer reports that her toe was cut with a file during the service, that she was told it was due to "thin skin," and that she was bleeding throughout the appointment. She felt the comment placed the responsibility on her.

When a client is injured, even slightly, the most reassuring response is immediate attention: stop the service, offer clean supplies, check that she is comfortable, and apologize sincerely. Technicians can reduce the chance of cuts by using a light touch with files, especially around the sides of the toes and the skin near the nail, and by asking whether the pressure is comfortable. Clients with sensitive skin are best served when a technician adapts to them, rather than attributing the concern to the client.

## Respecting a Client's "No"

The final concern involves an eyebrow service. The customer says the technician was very insistent that she needed it, and that she explained she already had an appointment elsewhere. She reports that when she tried to leave, the technician took her by the arm and guided her to the back of the salon, where she repeated that she had another appointment.

Offering additional services is part of salon work, but it should be done once, politely, and then dropped when the client declines. A client who says she has another appointment has given a complete and reasonable answer. Any physical contact beyond what a service requires can be unsettling, so teams should be trained to let clients move freely around the salon and to guide them only with words. The customer says the experience left her feeling taken advantage of, which is a reminder that a sale pursued after a clear no can cost a salon a long-term client.

## Editorial Perspective

Several lessons stand out from this account. First, confirm the service and the price before the appointment begins and again if anything changes, so the total never surprises a loyal client. Second, handle any cut or discomfort with immediate care and an apology, and avoid attributing the problem to the client. Third, accept a client's no at once and keep contact limited to the service, so that guests feel comfortable and free to leave on their own terms.
